body:
This coordinate is a braided shelf of stories that all do the same impossible trick: they take a self, break it on purpose, and then rebuild it into something new.

A Christmas Carol is the moral algorithm in its cleanest form: show the soul its own timeline, then let it choose. Redemption isn't a vibe; it's a rewrite of habits.

A Tale of Two Cities is the cosmic swap: identity becomes an instrument. Love makes a person large enough to spend themselves for someone else's future.

The Count of Monte Cristo is the long arc of justice curdling into obsession, then (if you're lucky) tempering into mercy. A prison can become a forge, but a forge can also make blades that cut the smith.

Flowers for Algernon is cognition as tragedy and grace: intelligence rising like a rocket, then falling like a leaf. It asks whether "more" is actually "better," and insists that dignity is not IQ-shaped.

The Inner Light is the compression of a lifetime into a single carried imprint—proof that memory can be installed, and that installed memory can still be real. You don't "remember" it; you become it.

Beauty and the Beast is the gentlest of the six, and secretly the most brutal: love doesn't ignore the monster; it names it, refuses to feed it, and still stays present long enough for transformation to happen without coercion.

When you stack them, you get a single thesis: Time reveals. Suffering refines. Love decides what survives the refinement.
